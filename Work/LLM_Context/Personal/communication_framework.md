# Communication best practice framework

Reference library of leadership communication practices. Used by the call-reflection skill to scan meeting transcripts and surface areas for growth. Practices here are the "broad net" - when one is repeatedly relevant, it gets promoted to a personal pattern in `growth_patterns.md`.

Each practice has observable signals that can be detected in a transcript.

---

## 1. Questioning

### 1.1 Open vs closed questions
**Best practice:** Use open questions (how, what, tell me about) to draw out thinking. Closed questions (did you, have you, is it) get yes/no answers and limit exploration.
**Signals in transcript:** Count ratio of open to closed questions. Flag if closed questions dominate, especially in coaching contexts.
**Exception:** Closed questions are appropriate for confirming facts or commitments ("Is the plan updated?" is fine).

### 1.2 One question at a time
**Best practice:** Ask a single question and wait. Stacking multiple questions lets the other person cherry-pick the easiest one.
**Signals in transcript:** Look for "?" appearing 2+ times in a single speaker turn without an intervening response.

### 1.3 Follow-up depth
**Best practice:** When someone gives a surface-level answer, follow up with "say more about that", "what specifically", "what makes you think that" rather than accepting the first answer.
**Signals in transcript:** After a short/vague response from the other person, does the leader probe deeper or move on?

### 1.4 Curiosity before judgement
**Best practice:** When someone presents an idea or approach, explore it before evaluating it. "Walk me through your thinking" before "I'm not sure that works."
**Signals in transcript:** Look for evaluation/judgement immediately following someone presenting an idea, without an exploratory question first.

## 2. Listening and space

### 2.1 Silence as a tool
**Best practice:** After asking a question, wait. Don't fill the silence. Discomfort with silence leads to answering your own question or rephrasing before the other person has processed.
**Signals in transcript:** Look for the leader asking a question, then speaking again before the other person responds (answering own question, rephrasing, adding context).

### 2.2 Completing their thought
**Best practice:** Let people finish their sentences and complete their ideas before responding. Don't jump in mid-thought.
**Signals in transcript:** Look for "Sorry keep going", "No go ahead", or interrupted turns where the leader starts speaking then yields.

### 2.3 Reflecting back
**Best practice:** Paraphrase what you heard before adding your perspective. "So what you're saying is..." This confirms understanding and makes the other person feel heard.
**Signals in transcript:** Does the leader acknowledge or reference what the other person just said before making their own point? Or do they pivot straight to their own view?

### 2.4 Acknowledging before redirecting
**Best practice:** When someone raises a point you want to redirect from, acknowledge it first ("I hear that", "That makes sense", "Good point") before pivoting. Going straight to the redirect feels dismissive.
**Signals in transcript:** Look for transitions where the leader moves to a new topic or challenges a point without first acknowledging what was said.

## 3. Feedback and challenge

### 3.1 Observation not judgement
**Best practice:** Frame feedback as observable behaviour, not character assessment. "The Friday summary wasn't delivered" not "You lack initiative." "I noticed X" not "You always Y."
**Signals in transcript:** Look for "you are" (identity) vs "I noticed" or "what happened with" (behaviour). Also watch for absolutes: "always", "never".

### 3.2 Specificity
**Best practice:** Feedback with a specific example lands. Feedback without one feels like opinion. "In Tuesday's email, the tone felt dismissive" vs "Your communication could be better."
**Signals in transcript:** Does feedback reference a specific instance, date, or deliverable? Or is it general?

### 3.3 Challenge through questions
**Best practice:** Instead of saying "that won't work", ask "what happens if X?" or "how would that play out with Y?" Lets the other person discover the issue themselves.
**Signals in transcript:** When disagreeing or seeing a problem, does the leader state the problem directly or use a question to surface it?

### 3.4 Balancing positive and developmental
**Best practice:** In ongoing relationships, note what's working as specifically as what needs to change. Not a "feedback sandwich" (that's performative), but genuine recognition alongside genuine challenge.
**Signals in transcript:** Is there any specific positive reinforcement in the conversation, or is it entirely developmental/corrective?

## 4. Coaching vs directing

### 4.1 Who owns the solution
**Best practice:** In coaching conversations, the other person should propose solutions. The leader validates, challenges, or refines - but doesn't generate.
**Signals in transcript:** When a problem or gap is identified, who speaks first with a proposed approach? If it's consistently the leader, that's directing.

### 4.2 Setting the bar vs setting the method
**Best practice:** "This needs to be at X standard by Y date" is leadership. "Do it this way" is micromanagement. Define the outcome, let them own the approach.
**Signals in transcript:** Look for prescriptive instructions (use this tool, talk to this person, structure it this way) vs outcome descriptions (I need to see X, it should achieve Y).

### 4.3 Resisting the rescue
**Best practice:** When someone is struggling or stuck, the temptation is to jump in and solve it. Better: "What have you tried?" or "What would you do if I wasn't here?"
**Signals in transcript:** When the other person expresses difficulty or uncertainty, does the leader provide the answer or ask what they've considered?

### 4.4 Checking for dependency
**Best practice:** If someone frequently asks "what do you think I should do?", that's a dependency signal. Reflect it back: "What are your options?" or "What's your instinct?"
**Signals in transcript:** Look for the other person deferring decisions or seeking approval. How does the leader respond - with an answer or a redirect?

## 5. Framing and structure

### 5.1 Bottom line up front
**Best practice:** Lead with the conclusion or ask, then provide supporting detail. Especially in time-pressured conversations. Don't build to the point - start with it.
**Signals in transcript:** Look for extended setup before the leader gets to their actual point or question.

### 5.2 One point at a time
**Best practice:** Make one point, check it's landed, then move to the next. Stacking multiple points in one turn dilutes all of them.
**Signals in transcript:** Extended leader turns covering 3+ distinct topics or points without checking understanding between them.

### 5.3 Explicit expectations
**Best practice:** End discussions of actions with clear who, what, by when. Don't assume shared understanding - state it.
**Signals in transcript:** Do action items have an explicit owner and deadline, or are they left vague ("we should probably", "it would be good to")?

### 5.4 Checking understanding
**Best practice:** After communicating something important, ask the other person to play it back. "What's your understanding of what we've agreed?" reveals gaps before they become problems.
**Signals in transcript:** After key discussions or decisions, does the leader check the other person's understanding or assume alignment?

## 6. Presence and adaptability

### 6.1 Matching energy
**Best practice:** Read the other person's energy and adapt. If they're frustrated, acknowledge it before problem-solving. If they're excited, don't dampen it with caveats. If they're flat, name it.
**Signals in transcript:** Look for emotional cues from the other person and how the leader responds - does the response match or ignore the emotional register?

### 6.2 Pace awareness
**Best practice:** Some people need time to process. If you're naturally fast, check yourself - are you outpacing them? Are they getting space to think?
**Signals in transcript:** Ratio of speaker turns. Very uneven turn length (long leader, short other) may indicate pacing mismatch.

### 6.3 Context switching
**Best practice:** When moving between topics, signal the transition clearly. "Let's shift to..." or "Different topic -". Abrupt shifts disorient.
**Signals in transcript:** Topic changes without a transition signal.

## 7. Influence and persuasion

### 7.1 Their frame, not yours
**Best practice:** Position ideas in terms of what matters to the other person, not what matters to you. "This will help you hit your targets" not "This is what I need."
**Signals in transcript:** When making a request or setting direction, is it framed around the leader's needs or the other person's goals/motivations?

### 7.2 Questions that lead to insight
**Best practice:** The most powerful coaching moments come from questions that make someone see something they hadn't seen. "How would you feel if someone asked you to sign that agreement?" is a great example - it reframes through empathy.
**Signals in transcript:** Look for perspective-shifting questions. Note these as positive examples to reinforce.

### 7.3 Building on their ideas
**Best practice:** "Yes, and..." is more powerful than "No, but...". Take what they've said and extend it rather than replacing it with your version.
**Signals in transcript:** When the other person proposes something, does the leader build on it or substitute their own approach?

## 8. Written strategic feedback (peers and cross-functional)

### 8.1 Lead with genuine acknowledgement
**Best practice:** Before any critique, name what's specifically strong about the work - not generic praise, but evidence you've actually read and understood it. This earns the right to challenge.
**Signals in written output:** Does the feedback open with a specific observation about what works ("the within-channel retention analysis gives us an evidence base we didn't have before") or with a generic compliment ("good work")?
**Why it matters:** People who've iterated multiple drafts need to know their effort landed before they can hear what needs to change.

### 8.2 Frame asks as opportunities, not corrections
**Best practice:** "I think there's an opportunity to..." or "I wonder if..." rather than "You need to..." or "This should be...". Especially important when the person doesn't report to you. The goal is to open a door, not issue an instruction.
**Signals in written output:** Count directive language ("you should", "this needs to") vs invitational language ("I wonder if", "there's an opportunity to", "I think this could"). The balance should lean heavily invitational for peer feedback.

### 8.3 Use metaphors to make structural feedback land
**Best practice:** Abstract structural feedback ("your strategy lacks specificity") is hard to act on. A concrete metaphor ("you describe the cuisine but don't name the dishes") makes the same point memorable and actionable. The best metaphors give the person a framework they can apply themselves.
**Signals in written output:** Is structural feedback accompanied by a metaphor or analogy that the reader can use as a thinking tool going forward?

### 8.4 Position as supporter, not authority
**Best practice:** When giving feedback to someone who doesn't report to you, close with an offer to help rather than an expectation to comply. "Happy to walk through this together" not "Let me know when this is updated." You're a thinking partner, not an approver.
**Signals in written output:** Does the closing offer collaboration or request compliance? Is the overall tone that of a peer who wants to help, or a senior who expects action?

### 8.5 Calibrate directness to the relationship
**Best practice:** Directness is a function of trust and reporting line. With direct reports, you can be more blunt. With peers or cross-functional partners, lead with empathy and frame feedback as shared problem-solving. Consider how many drafts they've already done and what constraints they're working within.
**Signals in written output:** Has the tone been consciously adjusted for the audience? Does the feedback acknowledge constraints the person faces (e.g. being a team of one, coordinating without authority, time pressure)?

### 8.6 Point to models, don't prescribe
**Best practice:** Rather than rewriting their work, point to examples that demonstrate what you're asking for. "Daniel's document does this well - the structure works" is more empowering than a detailed rewrite. Let them learn from the model and apply it their way.
**Signals in written output:** When suggesting improvements, does the feedback reference a concrete example the person can study, or does it provide a prescriptive rewrite?

### 8.7 Separate the TLDR from the detail
**Best practice:** For substantial written feedback, provide a short summary (3-4 lines) that captures the key messages, then offer the detail separately (thread, comment, document). Respects the reader's time and lets them choose how deep to go.
**Signals in written output:** Is there a clear summary that stands alone, with detail available but not forced on the reader?

## How this framework is used

### During call reflections
The call-reflection skill scans transcripts against both this framework and growth_patterns.md. Known patterns (growth_patterns.md) get detailed tracking. Framework practices get flagged when a notable instance is detected.

### Promoting to active tracking
When a framework practice appears repeatedly (3+ instances across different calls), the skill should suggest promoting it to a personal pattern in growth_patterns.md. The promotion includes real examples from Chris's calls, not just the generic description.

### Adding new practices
New practices can be added to this framework at any time. Sources: books, articles, coaching conversations, observed best practice in others. Use `add practice: [description]` to have it structured and added here.

### Framework is not a scorecard
Not every practice applies to every call. The skill should focus on the 2-3 most relevant observations per call, not exhaustively score all 20+ practices. Quality of insight matters more than coverage.
