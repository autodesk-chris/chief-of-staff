# Personal growth patterns

This file captures recurring patterns that Chris wants to improve. It also serves as a living log of growth-relevant moments - not just known patterns, but emerging ones too.

Agents should actively look for two things:
1. **Known patterns** - moments matching the documented patterns below
2. **New signals** - anything that suggests a growth area worth reflecting on (e.g., asking for help framing a message, reworking a document multiple times, hesitating on a decision, expressing frustration about a communication)

## Pattern 1: Sharing before aligning with peers

**What happens:** Tendency to move fast and share decisions or information before checking with leadership peers. Can cause friction when others feel bypassed or surprised.

**Example:** Shared that Ammar was becoming interim Engineering Manager before Oyvind had discussed the role with Ammar. Oyvind was very annoyed. (March 31, 2026)

**What good looks like:** Before sharing role changes, org decisions, or sensitive information - pause and ask: "Have all the relevant leaders been consulted/informed?"

**Trigger situations:**
- Announcing role changes or promotions
- Sharing decisions that affect other leaders' teams
- Communicating org changes in Slack or meetings

## Pattern 2: Slack communication in contentious discussions

**What happens:** In Slack threads with different points of view, tends to:
- Be too blunt
- Come across as defensive (without intending to)
- Not be as empathetic as intended

**Example:** (to be captured as instances occur)

**What good looks like:** In multi-viewpoint discussions, take a beat to consider how the message reads from the other person's perspective. Lead with acknowledgement before making your point.

**Trigger situations:**
- Slack threads with disagreement or debate
- Responding when feeling challenged
- Topics where people have strong opposing views

## Pattern 3: Doubling down on influencing instead of diagnosing disagreement

**What happens:** When someone in a peer or leadership setting doesn't seem to be "getting it", the instinct is to explain harder - repeat the point, add more detail, find another angle. But often the other person already understands; they just have a different viewpoint. The result is that you come across as trying to influence harder rather than listening to their actual position.

**Source:** Direct feedback from Carl (23 April 2026 - FY27 Goals meeting). He specifically called this out as a pattern he's noticed and wants Chris to be more conscious of.

**What good looks like:** When you feel the urge to re-explain, pause and ask: "Are we seeing this differently?" or "What's your concern with this approach?" Diagnose whether it's a comprehension gap or a genuine difference of opinion before responding.

**Trigger situations:**
- Leadership meetings with differing views
- Any situation where you feel someone "isn't getting it"
- Heated or time-pressured discussions where the instinct to influence kicks in
- Presenting a strategy or proposal that gets pushback

**Related to but distinct from:** Pattern 4 (over-explaining in coaching). This pattern is about peer/leadership dynamics, not coaching direct reports.

## Pattern 4: Over-explaining instead of coaching

**What happens:** When coaching or guiding someone, tendency to give extended explanations, metaphors, and walkthroughs when a shorter question would force the other person to think. The instinct is right (they need to hear it), but the length means teaching rather than testing.

**Examples:**
- Simon week 2 call (28 April 2026): Extended "busy hamster" metaphor explaining outcomes vs tasks. A shorter version - "What happens if you're busy all week but the numbers don't move?" - would force the same insight.
- Simon week 1 call (24 April 2026): Explained how to interpret run-rate metrics over 1.5 months, when a question like "How would you measure whether we're on track at week 3?" would test understanding.

**What good looks like:** Make the point in one sentence. If the other person doesn't get it, they'll ask. Shorter prompts force more thinking.

**Trigger situations:**
- 1:1s where someone isn't getting the concept
- Explaining frameworks or mental models
- Coaching conversations

---

## Pattern 5: Giving answers instead of asking questions

**What happens:** When someone presents a problem or gap, tendency to suggest specific tools, people, structures, or examples rather than asking them how they'd solve it. Often triggered when the other person invites guidance ("you might already have a few ideas").

**Examples:**
- Simon week 1 call (24 April 2026): Suggested Manus.ai, Joe as a US resource, the 10-creator model, and specific daily execution examples ("20 people by Tuesday, 5 conversations by Wednesday"). Simon left with a near-complete playbook he didn't build himself.
- Simon week 2 call (28 April 2026): Gave a full tutorial on AI agents (they're .md files, there's a repository, focus on repetitive work) when Simon asked for ideas. Better: "Have a look at the repository and come back with what you think could work."
- Simon week 2 call (28 April 2026): "Remember the baseline is just something to compare to... it doesn't have to be perfect, just consistent" - gave the answer. Better: "What would make this baseline useful for you week over week?"

**What good looks like:**
- **Ask, don't tell.** "How are you planning to build US market insight?" not "You should talk to Jo and use Manus."
- **Probe, don't solve.** "You've identified a data gap - what's your plan to close it?" not "Here's how I'd approach it."
- **Set the bar, not the method.** "I need to see week-by-week milestones" is fine. "Here's what Tuesday and Wednesday should look like" is doing their job.

**Trigger situations:**
- Someone presents a gap or problem without a proposed solution
- Someone directly asks "what would you do?" or "do you have ideas?"
- Coaching calls where the temptation is to fast-track their thinking
- When you can see the answer clearly and they can't (yet)

**Why it matters:**
- You can't assess independence if you give the answers
- It weakens any performance case if you're co-authoring their work
- It creates dependency rather than building capability

---

## Pattern 6: Airtime imbalance in 1:1s

**What happens:** In coaching and 1:1 conversations, tendency to take more airtime than necessary. This is often a byproduct of patterns 3 and 4 - over-explaining and giving answers both increase your share of the conversation.

**What good looks like:** In a coaching 1:1, the person being coached should be talking 60-70% of the time. Your role is to ask, probe, challenge, and confirm - not to teach.

**Trigger situations:**
- Any 1:1 where you're the senior person
- Coaching calls especially

## How agents should use this file

After generating the session summary, review the full conversation for:
- Moments matching known patterns above

- If nothing relevant, say nothing - no noise

### Daily summary
Review meeting notes and Slack context for pattern matches or new signals. Flag any relevant moments the same way.

- **Meeting prep:** When preparing for meetings where sensitive decisions will be discussed, remind about Pattern 1.
- **Document writing:** If Chris asks for help structuring or rewriting communications, note the context at session end.
