# My approach to product-led growth and experimentation

*Author: Chris Small. This is a working document that captures how I think about PLG and experimentation, drawn from the thesis I have argued at Autodesk Forma, the framework I have shared with leadership, and the operating principles I hold my teams to.*

---

## 1. What PLG is, and what it isn't

Product-led growth is not a project, not an experiment, and not something the Growth team does. It is a framework and a system that aligns the organisation around driving growth through the product itself. As I put it to Carl in October 2025: "PLG isn't a tactic or something owned by the Growth team. It's an organization-wide way of operating - a framework for how we build, deliver, and capture value through the product itself."

Without a shared framework, teams define PLG differently, work hard but not in sync, and make decisions that are inconsistent with being product-led. The symptom I see most often is linear rather than compounding growth: acquiring users but not building a habit.

## 2. Growth and Core are different jobs

Core product teams are built to create value. Product Growth teams are built to connect users to that value - to diagnose why users are not getting to value, remove the friction blocking them, and run experiments to accelerate adoption. When these jobs are conflated, growth work is what falls through the gap, because core is legitimately split between long-term platform work and short-term retention.

A dedicated growth function exists to absorb the short-term pressure so core can do its best long-term work. It needs its own engineering capacity, not just insight, because insights without execution capacity lose in core's prioritisation to the same long-term commitments that already squeeze short-term retention work.

Growth is a multiplier for core, not a competitor. Two mechanisms help make that real:

- Take on work core teams cannot get to. Parking in the US bundle is a good example of that in practice. 
- Ship for inheritance. Every experiment has a defined graduation path agreed with engineering leaders before it starts.

## 3. The shape of Growth depends on PMF

PMF is not a company-wide state. It is a property of a specific user segment. But recognising that must not become a licence to split focus. The discipline is to pick one user, the ideal user, and win with them first. Trying to win with multiple users at once dilutes focus and typically loses on all of them. Growth's mode at any given time is defined by which single user we are working to serve.

Where there is PMF with the user we are focused on, Growth's remit is the classic one: build the on-ramps, diagnose friction in the funnel, remove it, and run experiments to accelerate adoption across activation, retention, monetisation, and acquisition.

Where there is not PMF with that user, the remit shifts. Optimising funnels on surfaces that have not proven their value is low-impact work. The role becomes diagnostic and remedial: understand why users are not reaching value, run experiments to test hypotheses about the blockers, and ship targeted fixes for the gaps core has not prioritised. Successful fixes graduate to core (see the section on graduation).

Naming which mode we are operating in matters. Being honest about pre-PMF status prevents Growth from being measured against acquisition or funnel-conversion targets that were never the right lens for the moment.

## 4. Ideal user vs target user

The ideal user is not the same as the target user. Confusing the two is one of the most consequential mistakes in PLG.

- **Ideal user.** A user who is already getting value from the product as it exists today, gaps included. They are willing to pay for what they have. They use the product in spite of its gaps and recommend it to others. This is the user with whom we have product-market fit.
- **Target user.** The next user segment we are trying to win. Product-market fit with the target user is not yet established. Winning with the target user typically requires closing capability gaps, extending the workflow, or opening a new market position.

The bar for PMF is specific: usage of the current product, and willingness to pay for it in its current form. Not usage of a promised future version. Not willingness to pay after we close five gaps. Today's product, today's willingness.

Two implications:

- We must win with the ideal user first. That earns the right to move on to the target user. Trying to win with both at once splits the roadmap and typically loses on both.
- Winning with the ideal user does not mean the product is done. It usually means small tweaks to help more users who match the ideal profile recognise the product is for them, or to make it easier to slot the product into their workflow. It does not mean shipping new capabilities to reach a different segment.

## 5. The endgame

Winning with the ideal user needs to be defined concretely, not aspirationally. The endgame is the shared, measurable definition of what "won" looks like.

I hold the endgame to four tests:

- **Value.** A defined workflow the user completes end-to-end, with observable proof (analyses run, exports produced, artefacts shared).
- **Retention.** Repeat usage at a rate that matches or exceeds the best-performing comparable segment. For Forma, that means US retention on par with Norway on matched high-intent channels.
- **Willingness to pay.** Users converting from free to paid at a target rate for the current product, not the future one. Priced revenue, not signalled intent.
- **Referral.** Users recommending the product to peers, either through explicit advocacy or observable pass-through (invited collaborators, shared boards, community posts).

If all four hold for the ideal user, we have PMF with that segment and can move to the target user. If any of the four is missing, we are still in the diagnostic mode described in section 3, however healthy the vanity numbers look.

The endgame is written down. Every squad should be able to say which endgame criterion their current work is moving.

## 6. The monetisation model

The motion is free-to-paid. Users start free. Value is delivered before monetisation. Users convert to paid when they are getting enough value that paying is the natural next step.

Paid itself is a hybrid: subscription plus usage. Subscription is the foundation, giving access to the platform and its ongoing value. Usage is the accelerator, additional value paid for as users consume more of it.

Which specific capabilities sit in the subscription tier versus the usage tier, and where the free-to-paid gate falls, is not yet decided. That is a test-and-learn question. The commitment is to the shape of the model (free-to-paid, with hybrid paid), not yet to its wiring.

Two principles guide the testing:

- **Value before monetisation.** Users must experience enough value to be willing to pay before we ask them to. Gating value users have not yet experienced breaks the motion.
- **Alignment with usage.** Price should scale with the value the user is getting. Users doing more, getting more, and creating more artefacts should pay more. Users doing less should pay less or nothing. Anything else creates misalignment between price and value delivered.

## 7. Deliberate PLG

Deliberate PLG means understanding the specific mechanisms through which product drives growth, measuring them, and optimising them intentionally. Not "build a great product and hope growth follows."

My user journey has four stages, and each squad maps to a stage:

**Right intent -> First value moment -> Habitual usage -> Monetisation**

- Marketing and Community attract users with the right intent.
- First Strike designs the first value moment.
- User Engagement recovers users who fall off the path.
- Monetisation proves enough value has been delivered that users will pay.
- Strategic Accounts does the same for enterprise.

## 8. The bowling alley

The path to the first value moment is a bowling lane. The primary job is to design the lane so seamlessly that users stay on the path naturally. In our data, 70-85% of meaningful actions happen in the first session - users either experience value immediately or they effectively never do.

But users will fall off. That is what the bumpers are for. User Engagement is the bumper: AI-powered support, one-to-one engagement at scale, timely learning content, day 2-7 re-engagement.

## 9. Good friction versus bad friction

Remove friction that blocks value. Keep friction that enables it. Profiling around the user's outcome (not our internal categories) is good friction because it routes the right user to the right recipe. A generic tour that delays a user from doing the thing they came to do is bad friction.

## 10. How we deliver customer value: dishes, recipes, ingredients

Users have specific problems to solve. Can I fit 200 apartments on this site? Does this design meet shadow rules? Will this parking work? These are complete use cases with a beginning, middle, and end. Half a workflow is not value.

I use a food metaphor to make this concrete:

- **Ingredients** are capabilities. Solar analysis. Zoning data. DWG import.
- **Recipes** are the sequenced workflow that combines ingredients.
- **Dishes** are the presentable deliverable. What the architect hands to a client, submits to a city, or invoices for.

Our job is not just to create ingredients. It is to write the recipe and teach people how to cook the dish. Missing ingredients get added. Recipes get communicated. Dishes get validated with users.

Not every ingredient needs to be native. First-party extensions and third-party tools count. If the outcome can be delivered through the recipe and that is faster, we do that.

## 11. First strike must evolve per market

Our definition of first strike is stable: a user runs two analyses and sees enough value to return. What must evolve is what "analysis" means in context. Environmental analysis is the right first strike trigger in Norway. In the US, a shadow impact, parking, and financial feasibility are the analyses that matter. Same moment, different content.

## 12. Intent-driven acquisition

Not all acquisition is equal. Volume from low-intent channels is worth less than a smaller number of high-intent users. I bias toward high-intent acquisition - Product Center, Community, content that attracts users who already understand what we are offering - and treat low-intent channels as something to route carefully, not to scale indiscriminately.

## 13. How Growth and Core align: the Strategy Unit

Autonomy within alignment. Squads should be free to operate autonomously on execution, provided they are aligned on the destination and the principles that constrain the work. The Strategy Unit is the formal artefact: a cross-squad forum where Growth, Apps, and Tools squads meet to align on priorities, surface trade-offs, and resolve disagreement before it becomes friction.

Without it, adjacent work reads as encroachment. With it, teams can move independently in the confidence that they are pulling in the same direction.

## 14. How experiments graduate

An experiment is a learning vehicle, not a permanent feature. Three outcomes:

- **Successful experiments graduate to core ownership.** The receiving squad absorbs the feature into production on an agreed timeline. This protects Growth's velocity by keeping the team on new learning rather than accumulating maintenance.
- **Failed experiments are killed.** Code is removed. We do not keep it alive in hope.
- **Ambiguous outcomes get a decision, not drift.** The Strategy Unit or Squad is the forum to decide: kill, iterate, or graduate.

Growth signals readiness with evidence. Core confirms or pushes back. A handover is scheduled. Graduation is part of how Growth works, not an exception to it.

## 15. Experimentation: problem statements are the foundation

Everything downstream is only as good as the problem statement. A good problem statement:

- Describes a user's struggle or a gap in their behaviour, something real and observable.
- Is supported by data or solid qualitative evidence.
- Is completely solution-free. Product words - button, invite, modal, tour - are red flags.

The three stages I watch for:

- **Solution drift** ("many firms don't invite colleagues, so they miss collaboration benefits") - already prescribes the fix.
- **Motivation drift** ("users are reluctant to send invitations") - assumes an internal feeling that cannot be observed or measured.
- **Well-formed** ("most firms use the product with only one active user, rather than involving colleagues") - observable, measurable, leaves the solution space wide open.

**The three-solutions test.** Hand the statement to a team with no context. Ask them to brainstorm three genuinely different ways to solve it. If they can, the problem statement is doing its job. If they can only think of one, it is a solution disguised as a problem.

## 16. Hypothesis and prediction

Once the problem is clean, the hypothesis and prediction fall out of it. I use a strict format:

**Hypothesis** - the belief we are testing.
- Written from the user's point of view, not the company's.
- Uses "We believe...". Avoids "if", "could", "should".
- No solution or feature framing.
- Connects to the root cause and to supporting data, without needing to cite specific numbers in the statement itself.
- Falsifiable - the belief can be proven wrong.

**Prediction** - what we expect to see if the hypothesis holds.
- Written as "If... then...".
- Framed at the outcome level, so multiple solution variations could test the same prediction.
- Observable and falsifiable, even before metrics are fully defined.

The pair matters. The hypothesis is what we are learning about the user. The prediction is what we would see if we are right. Keeping them separate stops us from testing a solution and calling it a learning.

## 17. Experimentation 

- A 60-70% failure rate is a healthy ambition indicator. If everything works, we are not being ambitious enough.
- We do not need statistical significance to move. "More likely than not" is good enough given our user volumes. We inform decisions through directional data, product intuition, user feedback, and product sense.
- We distinguish experiments (we do not know the answer) from initiatives (we know what to build). Being honest about which is which prevents us from treating an experiment like a commitment or an initiative like a question.
- Extensions and experiments are disposable learning assets, not legacy code. They can ship in days, not weeks. Risk is isolated. When something is not working, kill it, clean it up, and move on. 

## 18. Operating principles

- **Coach the action, not the result.** Obsess over whether we are running the right experiments, asking the right questions, building the right things, and learning from what we ship. Get the actions right and the results follow.
- **Data honesty.** Start from the data, not from opinions looking for data to confirm them.
- **Problem-space thinking.** Our problem statements should be about users and their struggles, not features and UI elements.
- **Bravery.** Swing big, scope small. Try things that might not work. Share failures as openly as successes.
- **Constraints as design parameters.** Small squads leveraging agents are a forcing function for better thinking, not a compromise.
- **Agentic-first as default.** Start with agents. The old way is the fallback, not the plan.

## 19. What we are choosing not to do

- Not optimising for low-intent users while high-intent users still fail to reach value.
- Not building every feature natively when extensions can validate demand faster.
- Not waiting for other teams. If a gap matters and core cannot get to it in the window, we solve it and share what we learn.
- Not treating experiments as permanent. We kill what does not work and clean up the code.
- Not measuring MAU alone. MAU without habit is a vanity metric.
